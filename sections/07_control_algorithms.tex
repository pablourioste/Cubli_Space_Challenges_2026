% ================= 7. CONTROL ALGORITHMS & APPROACH =================
\section{Control Algorithms \& Approach}
\label{sec:control_algorithms}

This section derives the balancing controller from the linear models of
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, states the controller as executable logic rather
than only as equations, describes the Simulink/Simscape environment used to
exercise that logic against both prototypes' CAD geometry before it runs on
hardware, and gives the onboard software architecture that carries it at
\qty{1}{\kilo\hertz} in flight. Detailed pseudocode, the fixed gains and
tuning parameters, the timing budget and the fault responses are collected in
Appendix~\ref{app:control_algorithms}.

\subsection{LQR Baseline Control}
\label{sec:control2d}

Linearising about the upright equilibrium $(\theta_b,\dot\theta_b,\dot\theta_w)=(0,0,0)$ gives
\begin{equation}
\dot x(t) = Ax(t) + Bu(t), \qquad x := (\theta_b,\dot\theta_b,\dot\theta_w),
\end{equation}
with $A, B$ built from the parameters in Table~\ref{tab:2d_vars}.
Zero-order-hold discretization at a rate set by the open-loop unstable pole,
with a safety margin, gives $x[k+1] = A_dx[k]+B_du[k]$. A discrete LQR gain
$K_d=(K_{d1},K_{d2},K_{d3})$ minimises
\begin{equation}
J(u) = \sum_k x^{\mathrm T}[k]Qx[k] + u^{\mathrm T}[k]Ru[k],
\end{equation}
giving $u[k]=-K_d(\hat\theta_b[k],\hat{\dot\theta}_b[k],\hat{\dot\theta}_w[k])$.
The same design generalises directly to the corner-balancing 9-state model of
Section~\ref{sec:3dmodel}: $K_d$ becomes a $3\times9$ gain matrix, using the
same discretization and cost function, with $Q, R$ weighting all three tilt
axes, body rates and wheel speeds, and $\hat\theta_b$ replaced by the
reduced-attitude estimate $\phi$ derived below.

\paragraph{The estimate the controller actually needs.} $\theta_b$ is not
measured directly. What the IMU gives is a gyroscope rate and an
accelerometer reading, and the accelerometer measures \emph{specific force},
not gravity: at radius $r$ from the pivot it also picks up the tangential
($r\ddot\theta_b$) and centripetal ($r\dot\theta_b^2$) acceleration induced by
the plate's own rotation, both largest during exactly the fast recoveries the
controller most needs a good estimate for. Two more properties of the raw
signal matter once the estimate is built:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a fixed instrument angle such as $\tan^{-1}(-a_x/a_y)$ is only a
        correct measure of tilt \emph{at} the equilibrium the arctangent was
        set up around; away from it, on the cube, it mixes the unobservable
        yaw direction into the tilt estimate the moment the body rotates
        about more than one axis;
  \item any such angle, on the cube, is one instance of a three-parameter
        chart of $SO(3)$, and no such chart is free of a singularity
        \cite{stuelpnagel1964parametrization}. A representation that is well behaved
        near one equilibrium is not guaranteed to stay well behaved 50--125\textdegree{}
        away from it, which is the range spanned by the eight corner
        equilibria of the cube.
\end{itemize}
Both problems are structural, not tuning problems, and both are removed by
building the estimate as a vector quantity rather than an angle.

\paragraph{Reduced-attitude estimate.} Let $\hat\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^3$ be the
filter's running estimate of the body-frame gravity direction --- the sensor
realisation of $R^{\mathrm T}\hat g$ in the gravity-torque term $\tau_g$ of
Table~\ref{tab:3d_vars} --- and let $\hat g_B$ be its value at the balanced
attitude, a constant fixed once by the corner (or edge) geometry and distinct
from the world-frame $\hat g$ of Table~\ref{tab:3d_vars}. With the calibrated,
mounting-rotated accelerometer reading $a_b$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:mount_rotation}:
\begin{align}
\gamma_a[k] &= \mathrm{normalize}(-a_b[k]) &&\text{instantaneous, accelerometer-only} \label{eq:gamma_a}\\
e[k] &= \hat\gamma[k]\times\gamma_a[k] &&\text{correction vector} \label{eq:cf_error}\\
\hat\gamma[k+1] &= \mathrm{normalize}\Big(\hat\gamma[k] + \Delta t\big({-}\,\omega_c[k]\times\hat\gamma[k] + k_P\,e[k]\times\hat\gamma[k]\big)\Big) &&\text{propagate + correct} \label{eq:complementary}\\
\hat b[k+1] &= \hat b[k] + k_I\,e[k]\,\Delta t &&\text{gyro-bias integral} \label{eq:bias_integral}\\
\omega_c[k] &= \omega_b[k] - \hat b[k] &&\text{bias-corrected body rate}
\end{align}
with $\hat\gamma[0]=\hat g_B$, $\hat b[0]=\mathbf 0$, and fixed gains
$k_P=5$, $k_I=0.5$ (the $\omega_c$ used in Equation~\eqref{eq:complementary}
is the previous cycle's estimate; both update simultaneously each tick). The
entire attitude computation the controller feeds on is then one cross
product,
\begin{equation}
\phi[k] = -\hat g_B\times\hat\gamma[k],
\label{eq:reduced_attitude}
\end{equation}
which is, by construction, exact and singularity-free at any attitude short
of the antipode $\hat\gamma=-\hat g_B$ -- a configuration reached only by
picking the cube up and inverting it, well outside the range over which the
controller is armed (fault handling, Section~\ref{sec:control_sw}). On the
2D rig, motion is confined to the plane spanned by the pivot axis, so
$\theta_b$ is recovered as a single component,
\begin{equation}
\theta_b[k] := \phi[k]\cdot e_{\text{pivot}},
\end{equation}
rather than by a separate estimator; the 2D and 3D cases share one
construction, not two.

\paragraph{Why this is the estimate to use, not $\tan^{-1}(\cdot)$ corrected
after the fact.} Equation~\eqref{eq:reduced_attitude} is a cross product with
$\hat g_B$, which is orthogonal to $\hat g_B$ by construction at every
attitude and every tilt magnitude -- not only to first order. It therefore
cannot represent the unobservable yaw component of the estimate at all, which
is the property a raw Euler-style angle does not have and cannot be given by
a correction applied after the fact. The construction is the passive form of
the complementary filter of \citet{mahony2008nonlinear}, specialised to a
single vector measurement.

\paragraph{Mounting position.} An accelerometer away from the pivot reads the
lever-arm terms above as a transient during a recovery, not as a
mounting-dependent bias: the low-pass action of
Equations~\eqref{eq:complementary}--\eqref{eq:cf_error} is approximately an
integral, and $\int \ddot\theta_b\,\mathrm dt$ across a completed recovery is
the change in $\dot\theta_b$, which is zero once the rig is at rest again. On
the 2D rig this cancellation is measured to hold from $r=0$ to
\qty{106}{\milli\metre}, with the residual error floor set by gyro bias
($k_I$-scaled) rather than by $r$: mounting position is not the constraint on
the panel that it first appears to be, and the BMI270 is placed by wiring and
packaging convenience (Section~\ref{sec:imu_mount}), not by a lever-arm
requirement.

The same cancellation does not transfer unchanged to the cube. One
accelerometer term the panel can neglect -- the centripetal component
$\omega\times(\omega\times r)$, quadratic in $\omega$ and always directed
inward -- has a DC component in three dimensions and does not average out,
so it is corrected explicitly rather than relied on to cancel. More
consequentially, the cancellation itself has a measured range: mounted at
the primary balancing corner, recovery holds; mounted past roughly
\qty{150}{\milli\metre} from a given corner, the antipodal corner (at
\qty{260}{\milli\metre} on this cube) falls outside the range over which
Equation~\eqref{eq:complementary} still rejects the lever-arm term, and the
estimate for that corner degrades sharply rather than gracefully. The design
rule that follows is geometric, not electrical: keep the IMU within
approximately \qty{150}{\milli\metre} of every corner the cube is meant to
balance on -- satisfied by a near-geometric-centre placement, and the reason
balancing on more than one corner stays compatible with a single,
once-calibrated IMU rather than a per-corner sensor recalibration.

\paragraph{The estimator ladder.} $k_P=5$, $k_I=0.5$, gyro-integration-only
between corrections is the estimator flown for corner balancing, chosen
because it is the simplest structure that removes both problems above and
its recovery cost is small once the sensor is calibrated at startup. It is
the first rung of a ladder, not a final answer:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Reduced-attitude complementary filter with bias}
        (Equations~\eqref{eq:gamma_a}--\eqref{eq:reduced_attitude}) -- flown now.
  \item \textbf{Steady-state linear Kalman filter} on $[\phi;\,\omega;\,\hat b_{\text{gyro}}]$
        -- the model-based upgrade: $\phi$ is already a tangent-plane vector,
        not an $SO(3)$ chart, so the usual objection to a linear filter on
        attitude does not apply here. A constant gain matrix keeps the
        runtime cost identical to Equation~\eqref{eq:complementary}, and the
        filter reconstructs the lever-arm term from the model rather than
        relying on it cancelling, which removes most of the residual phase
        lag between the true and filtered tilt.
  \item \textbf{Disturbance-torque state augmentation} on top of the KF,
        giving integral action against unmodelled torques for free.
  \item \textbf{Multiplicative EKF}, with the gyro bias estimated online --
        deferred to Stage~3, and adopted only if bench data shows thermal
        drift inside the closed cube (three motors, one enclosure) that the
        once-at-startup calibration below does not cover.
\end{enumerate}
Startup calibration already removes most of the available headroom before
any of rungs 2--4 are needed: an uncalibrated gyro bias of
\qty{1}{\degree\per\second} costs roughly \qty{27}{\percent} of the wheel
momentum cap as a standing offset (Section~\ref{sec:3dmodel}), a
once-at-startup calibration reduces that to \qty{1.4}{\percent}, and rung 4
would buy roughly another factor of five on top of a number that is already
small. Rung 2 is the next step taken on this project, not because rung 1 is
inadequate for the pass criterion, but because it is the cheapest remaining
reduction in the residual phase lag noted below.

\paragraph{Cube versus panel: the cancellation is smaller, not absent.}
Section~\ref{sec:3dmodel}'s corner-balance case shows a real, but smaller,
version of the same effect: raw-accelerometer tilt error against the
reduced-attitude filter of Equation~\eqref{eq:complementary} falls from
\qty{3.42}{\degree} to \qty{2.75}{\degree}, a \qty{20}{\percent} penalty,
against \qty{0.5}{\percent} on the 2D rig. The gap is not lever-arm error --
mounting inside the \qty{150}{\milli\metre} radius above removes that -- and
not measurement noise; it is phase lag, because
Equation~\eqref{eq:complementary} is model-free and can only integrate the
gyro between corrections, and closing that gap is exactly what rung 2 of the
ladder is for.

This fixed-gain design is the baseline balancing controller;
Table~\ref{tab:control2d_vars} summarizes the control variables.

\subsection{IMU-to-Body Frame Transformation}
\label{sec:imu_mount}

The BMI270 cannot be mounted with its own sensor axes aligned to the body
frame $\{B\}$ of Figure~\ref{fig:cubli_3dof}: it sits on Perfboard~1 next to
the flight computer, placed for wiring and packaging rather than axis
alignment (Section~\ref{sec:layout}) -- a placement Section~\ref{sec:control2d}
shows is not constrained by the lever-arm concern that would otherwise argue
for mounting at the pivot. Every raw gyro/accel sample is therefore
rotated into $\{B\}$ by a fixed mounting matrix before it reaches the
complementary filter of Equation~\eqref{eq:complementary} or the estimator
ladder of Section~\ref{sec:control2d}.

Raw samples are first put in physical units in the sensor's own frame,
\begin{equation}
a_{s,i} = \frac{g_0\,\bar a_{\mathrm{raw},i} - a_{\mathrm{off},i}}{a_{\mathrm{scale},i}},
\qquad
\omega_{s,i} = \frac{\pi}{180}\,\bar\omega_{\mathrm{raw},i} - \omega_{\mathrm{bias},i},
\qquad i=1,2,3,
\label{eq:imu_calib}
\end{equation}
with $g_0=\qty{9.80665}{\metre\per\second\squared}$ and the bias/offset/scale
constants of Table~\ref{tab:imu_calib}, fixed at calibration and not
re-estimated online. The sensor-frame vectors are then rotated into the body
frame by the fixed mounting matrix $C_{SB}$,
\begin{equation}
\omega_b = C_{SB}\,\omega_s, \qquad a_b = C_{SB}\,a_s,
\label{eq:mount_rotation}
\end{equation}
where $C_{SB}$ is the classical Z-X-Z (3-1-3) proper Euler-angle rotation
matrix built from three fixed mounting angles $(\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3)$,
set once from the as-built mounting geometry:
\begin{equation}
C_{SB} =
\begin{bmatrix}
c_1c_3-c_2s_1s_3 & c_3s_1+c_1c_2s_3 & s_2s_3\\[2pt]
-c_1s_3-c_2c_3s_1 & c_1c_2c_3-s_1s_3 & c_3s_2\\[2pt]
s_1s_2 & -c_1s_2 & c_2
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad c_i=\cos\theta_i,\ s_i=\sin\theta_i,
\label{eq:mount_dcm}
\end{equation}
with $(\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3) = (\qty{-30}{\degree},\ \qty{54.7356}{\degree},\ \qty{45}{\degree})$.
The middle angle is exactly $\arccos(1/\sqrt3)$, the angle between a cube edge
and its space diagonal, consistent with a package mounted flush to a face
while the axis the controller cares about at corner-up runs along the body
diagonal from $O$. Unlike the attitude \emph{estimate} of
Section~\ref{sec:control2d}, $C_{SB}$ is a single, fixed rotation computed
once from as-built geometry rather than tracked over a trajectory, so the
usual objection to a three-angle parameterisation (Section~\ref{sec:control2d})
does not apply to it: there is no trajectory here to encounter a singularity
on. $C_{SB}$ is nonetheless verified orthonormal (unit determinant, unit row
norms) whenever the mounting angles are set, guarding against a
transcription error in $(\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3)$.

\subsection{Gain Scheduling and Mode Transitions}
\label{sec:gain_scheduling}

The controller switches from the open-loop jump-up sequence to the LQR law
once $\|\hat\phi\| < \theta_{\text{th}}$ ($|\hat\theta_b|<\theta_{\text{th}}$
on the 2D rig), with hysteresis around $\theta_{\text{th}}$ to prevent mode
chattering. The baseline uses a single fixed gain $K_d$ at the upright
equilibrium. If recovery degrades near the edges of the operating range,
where the small-angle linearisation weakens, gain scheduling will be added:
additional gains $K_d^{(i)}$ computed at several linearisation points, with
the controller interpolating between them based on $\hat\phi$, reusing the
same discrete structure. The mode-transition logic gains a third mode for the
3D cube, Edge-to-Corner jump-up, triggered once edge balancing is stable,
followed by a second threshold switch into the corner LQR once the estimated
orientation nears corner-up; the full state machine is given in
Table~\ref{tab:sw_modes}.

\subsection{Pure LQR Control Logic}
\label{sec:pure_lqr_logic}

Section~\ref{sec:control2d} derives $K_d$ offline, once, from the linear model.
What actually executes on the flight computer every control tick is the
sequence below, stated independent of how $K_d$ was computed so that the
running controller can be read, checked and re-implemented without
re-deriving the gain. Table~\ref{tab:control2d_vars} (2D) and its 3D
counterpart of Section~\ref{sec:3dmodel} define every symbol used; the fully
detailed pseudocode, including the supervisory state machine, is in
Appendix~\ref{app:control_algorithms}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sense.} Read raw gyro rate and accelerometer $a_s$ from the
        IMU over SPI; read wheel angle(s) from the encoder(s) over the
        moteus CAN reply.
  \item \textbf{Rotate into body axes.} Apply the calibration of
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:imu_calib} and the mounting rotation
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:mount_rotation} to obtain $\omega_b[k],\,a_b[k]$.
  \item \textbf{Estimate attitude.} Update the complementary filter,
        Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gamma_a}--\eqref{eq:bias_integral}, to obtain
        $\hat\gamma[k]$ and $\hat b[k]$, then form $\phi[k]$
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_attitude}) and the bias-corrected rate
        $\omega_c[k]$.
  \item \textbf{Assemble the state.} Build
        $\hat x[k] = (\theta_b[k],\ \hat{\dot\theta}_b[k],\ \hat{\dot\theta}_w[k])$
        in 2D, or the 9-vector $(\phi[k],\omega_c[k],\omega_w[k])$ of
        Section~\ref{sec:3dmodel} in 3D.
  \item \textbf{Check the mode.} If the supervisory state machine
        (Table~\ref{tab:sw_modes}) is not in a balancing mode, skip to
        step~8 and issue that mode's own open-loop command instead of
        $u[k]$ below.
  \item \textbf{Compute the command.} $u[k] = -K_d\,\hat x[k]$.
  \item \textbf{Saturate and manage momentum.} Clip $u[k]$ to the driver's
        continuous current rating (Appendix~\ref{app:bom}); if a wheel speed
        $\hat{\dot\theta}_w$ exceeds its desaturation threshold, add a small
        bias torque directed to bleed the stored momentum without
        materially perturbing $\phi$.
  \item \textbf{Actuate.} Issue $u[k]$ (or the mode's open-loop command) as a
        CAN-FD torque request to each moteus driver.
  \item \textbf{Advance.} $k \leftarrow k+1$; repeat at the next
        \qty{1}{\kilo\hertz} tick (Section~\ref{sec:software_pipeline}).
\end{enumerate}

This is the loop exercised in simulation (Section~\ref{sec:sim_environment})
before it is exercised on hardware, and it is the loop whose worst-case
timing is bounded by construction in Section~\ref{sec:software_pipeline}.

\subsection{Simulation Environment: Simulink/Simscape Multibody}
\label{sec:sim_environment}

The control law of Sections~\ref{sec:control2d}--\ref{sec:pure_lqr_logic} is
validated in simulation before any closed-loop hardware run, on a plant built
from the same CAD geometry as the physical prototypes rather than from the
lumped-parameter equations alone --- so that the simulation catches geometry
effects (asymmetric mass distribution, off-axis clearances) the analytic model
cannot represent.

\paragraph{CAD-to-Simscape workflow.} Each CAD assembly --- the 1-DoF rig of
Section~\ref{sec:cad_2d} and the 3D cube of Section~\ref{sec:mechanical} --- is
exported as a STEP assembly and imported into Simscape Multibody, which
converts each part and mate into a rigid body and joint in a multibody tree.
The pivot bearing of the 1-DoF rig and the corner/edge contact features of the
3D cube are modelled as the corresponding revolute/point-contact joints, so
that the simulated degrees of freedom match Sections~\ref{sec:2dmodel}
and~\ref{sec:3dmodel} exactly rather than approximating them. Per-part mass and
inertia are taken directly from the CAD model, which is the same convention
that makes the mass roll-up of Section~\ref{sec:cad_method} auditable: the
simulation, the sizing, and the fabricated part all trace to one model.

\paragraph{Actuation and sensing blocks.} Each motor is represented as a
torque source at its joint, saturated and rate-limited to the envelope of
Section~\ref{sec:electrical}; the brake is a discrete event that zeroes the
relevant wheel-body relative velocity over the $\Delta t$ of
Equation~\eqref{eq:tau_b_from_dt}. Simulated IMU and encoder blocks apply
noise and bias models set from the sensors' characterised behaviour once
measured on hardware, so that the same estimator and control logic of
Section~\ref{sec:pure_lqr_logic} --- not a re-implementation of it --- runs
against simulated sensor data in a controller-in-the-loop configuration
before it runs against real sensors.

\paragraph{Test cases.} Three cases are run against the LQR baseline: recovery
from an initial tilt offset, which establishes the region of attraction;
rejection of an impulse disturbance applied at the body, which is the
simulated counterpart of the disturbance-recovery criterion the hardware is
later judged against; and behaviour under wheel-speed saturation, which
exercises the momentum-dumping step of Section~\ref{sec:pure_lqr_logic} and is
the case most likely to be missed by a purely linear analysis, since
saturation is precisely where the linear model ceases to describe the plant.
Each case is re-run with the plant parameters perturbed about their nominal
values, by a magnitude set once the identification tolerances of
Section~\ref{sec:paramid} are measured, so that the gains are shown not to be
knife-edge: a gain set that stabilises only the nominal plant is not a result,
because the nominal plant is not the one that will be built.

\paragraph{Direction of validation.} The measured parameters of
Section~\ref{sec:paramid} are substituted into the plant and the gains
re-derived before hardware balance is attempted; the simulation is validated
against the measured plant, never the reverse. A discrepancy between
simulated and measured closed-loop behaviour is treated as a finding about
the model.

\paragraph{Status.} The 2D rig's Simscape model, plant and gains are validated
on hardware. On the 3D cube, the plant is measured and the gains are
validated against a set of independent numerical gates run before any result
built on the model is trusted -- linearisation against the hand-derived $A,B$
chief among them -- with the corner-contact geometry modelled as a Gimbal
joint. That joint passed every equilibrium and linearisation gate and then
failed in closed-loop simulation, away from the equilibrium the gates check:
the middle gimbal primitive diverged numerically during a fast recovery, the
signature of the joint's own parameterisation going singular rather than of
the plant. Gate coverage at the equilibrium is therefore not coverage of
every failure mode the estimator or the plant model can exhibit, and a
Spherical-joint rebuild, which carries no such chart, is in progress for the
final validated model. Hardware bring-up on the 3D cube is under way in
parallel.

\subsection{System Integration}
\label{sec:integration}

\subsubsection{Onboard Software Pipeline}
\label{sec:software_pipeline}

The subsystems of Figure~\ref{fig:system_loop} are coordinated by a single
deterministic task on the Teensy, whose structure is given in
Figure~\ref{fig:software_pipeline}. Each \qty{1}{\milli\second} cycle executes
the same fixed sequence: sensor ingest reads the BMI270 over SPI and the moteus
replies over CAN; the estimator of Section~\ref{sec:control2d} propagates
$\hat\gamma$ on the gyro and corrects it against the accelerometer while
integrating gyro bias; the state vector $x = [\phi,\ \omega_c,\ \omega_w]$
is assembled from the estimator output and the wheel speeds; the LQR law of
Section~\ref{sec:control2d} evaluates $u = -Kx$ with a penalty on stored
wheel momentum to bleed off wheel saturation; and the resulting commands
leave as CAN torque requests to the drivers and a PWM signal to the brake
servos. Telemetry is tapped off the assembled state to SD and Wi-Fi outside
the control path, so logging cannot perturb loop timing.

The fixed ordering matters: with no dynamic allocation and no blocking calls, the
worst-case cycle time is bounded by construction rather than measured after the
fact, which is what makes the \qty{1}{\kilo\hertz} sample period of the discrete
LQR design a design guarantee rather than an average.

\subsubsection{Control Software Architecture}
\label{sec:control_sw}

Sections~\ref{sec:control2d}--\ref{sec:pure_lqr_logic} derive the control law
and its executable logic; this subsection states how that law is realised as
software, which is a separate set of decisions. The implementation detail ---
the mode state machine, the estimator and controller in executable
pseudocode, the fixed gains and tuning parameters, the timing budget and the
fault responses --- is collected in Appendix~\ref{app:control_algorithms}, so
that a re-tuned gain does not require an edit to the design sections.

The software is organised in the four layers of
Table~\ref{tab:sw_layers}. The division is not stylistic: each layer has a
different failure mode and a different verification method, and the interfaces
between them are what allow the controller validated in simulation
(Section~\ref{sec:sim_environment}) to be the same code that runs on the cube.

\paragraph{Operating modes.} The supervisory layer sequences the modes of
Table~\ref{tab:sw_modes}, with hysteresis on every threshold to prevent
chattering at a boundary, and with a disarm transition available from every
mode. The modes are specified as a state machine because the critical
transitions are the descending ones: a fall detected during corner balance must
reach a safe state with the wheels commanded down, not merely switch to a
different gain.

\paragraph{Real-time guarantees.} The loop-rate claim of
Section~\ref{sec:control2d} is a design guarantee only if the worst-case cycle
is bounded by construction, which is why the pipeline of
Figure~\ref{fig:software_pipeline} uses a fixed stage ordering with no dynamic
allocation and no blocking calls, and why telemetry is a side-branch rather than
a stage.

\paragraph{Fault handling.} The failure cases the software must survive are
sensor dropout, bus error, wheel saturation, loss of the balancing
equilibrium, and the attitude estimate approaching the antipode noted in
Section~\ref{sec:control2d}. The first three, and the antipode case, have
defined responses that keep the machine controlled or bring it to a
controlled disarm; loss of the balancing equilibrium is a controlled shutdown
rather than a recovery attempt, since a cube that has left its capture region
cannot be recovered by the linear law and continued actuation only adds
energy to the fall.
