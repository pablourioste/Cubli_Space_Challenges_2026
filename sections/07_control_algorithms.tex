% ================= 7. CONTROL ALGORITHMS & APPROACH =================
\section{Control Algorithms \& Approach}
\label{sec:control_algorithms}

\subsection{What has to be regulated}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Balanced on a corner, the cube is an inverted pendulum in every direction
perpendicular to the vertical. Linearised about the balancing attitude it has
two unstable eigenvalues,
%
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{1,2} = 8.2572,\; 8.2343\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},
  \qquad
  \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{S_g}{\bar{\bm{\Theta}}}},
  \label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
%
a divergence time constant of 121\,ms. Nothing in the open-loop plant resists
displacement: the gravitational moment grows with tilt rather than opposing
it. The controller supplies all of the stability there is.

Three properties of the plant set the shape of the control problem, and each
is addressed by a specific part of what follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The attitude state is an element of $SO(3)$, not a vector.}
        No three-parameter global parameterisation of $SO(3)$ exists without
        a singularity, and the operating point sits $53.94^\circ$ from the
        orientation in which the geometry was authored, so small-angle
        reasoning about the parameterisation itself is not available
        (\S\ref{sec:attitude-representation}).
  \item \textbf{One rotational degree of freedom is degenerate at both ends.}
        Gravity produces no moment about the vertical, and a single vector
        measurement cannot observe rotation about it. The problem is
        eight-dimensional, not nine (\S\ref{sec:structural}).
  \item \textbf{Authority is bounded by stored momentum, not by torque.}
        The wheels exchange momentum with the body rather than pushing
        against the world, so recovery consumes wheel momentum and the
        envelope is set by what the wheels can store before saturating
        (\S\ref{sec:nonlinearities}).
\end{enumerate}

\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{Attitude representation}
\label{sec:attitude-representation}

This is the load-bearing decision of the control design. Three
representations were implemented and instrumented before the loop closed; two
failed in ways that resembled tuning problems and were not.

\subsubsection{Euler angles: three measured failures}
\label{sec:euler-failures}

The first implementation used a $Z$--$Y$--$X$ Euler triple. It failed three
times, in three distinct ways.

\paragraph{Failure 1 --- the parameterisation went singular, after passing
four gates.} The Simscape plant was built with a Gimbal joint on defensible
grounds: it linearises to a clean $9\times9$ system comparable directly
against the hand-derived $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$, the equilibrium gimbal angles
are $[0,0,0]$ because the mount transform pre-applies the corner tilt, and
lock sits roughly $75^\circ$ away at corner-balance amplitudes. It passed
validation gates~1 to~4 on that basis, including the linearisation gate at
$\lVert\Delta\bm{A}\rVert = 1.742\times10^{-7}$,
$\lVert\Delta\bm{B}\rVert = 2.157\times10^{-16}$ (measured on the
$(+1,+1,+1)$ configuration; see \S\ref{sec:validation}).

It failed later, in closed-loop simulation, with a diagnostic signature:
$q_x$ and $q_z$ smooth throughout, while $q_y$ --- the middle gimbal
primitive, the one carrying the singularity --- spiked to $\pm20000^\circ$
and returned within milliseconds. A rigid body cannot rotate that far in that
interval, and the confinement of the excursion to the middle primitive
identifies the cause exactly: the Jacobian relating gimbal rates to body
rates diverging, with the solver integrating the divergence.

It also retrospectively explained a persistent $19.8\,\%$ error in the
gravity block of $\bm{A}$ that never resolved while $\bm{B}$ stayed exact at
$3\times10^{-16}$. That asymmetry is the tell: $\bm{B}$ does not involve the
attitude parameterisation and $\bm{A}$ does, so a discrepancy appearing in
one and not the other is a coordinates error rather than a mass-properties
or wiring error.

The remedy is a Spherical joint, which carries no chart at the cost of a
rank-deficient linearisation.

\paragraph{Failure 2 --- an Euler triple is not the reduced attitude.} Even
far from lock, the triple $[q_1,q_2,q_3]$ is not a rotation vector, so
projecting out the vertical component removes yaw only to first order.

On this cube yaw is not small, for a mechanical reason. The inertia about the
vertical is
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\bm{g}}_B^{\top}\bm{\Theta}\,\hat{\bm{g}}_B
  = 5.374\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},
\end{equation}
%
against a mean tilt inertia of $2.825\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ in the
plane perpendicular to $\hat{\bm{g}}_B$ --- the cube is $5.3\times$ lighter in
yaw than in tilt. Wheel momentum along $\hat{\bm{g}}_B$ therefore spins the
body at $I_s/(\hat{\bm{g}}_B^{\top}\bm{\Theta}\hat{\bm{g}}_B)
= 0.0906\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ per $\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ of
$\bm{\rho}\cdot\hat{\bm{g}}_B$. Released from $3.6^\circ$, yaw reaches
$23.6^\circ$ within $0.3\,$s --- entirely physical and
momentum-conserving, and far outside the range where a first-order yaw
removal is valid. Feeding the Euler triple to the controller cost
$19.5\,\%$ of the recovery angle, and the loop diverged.

\paragraph{Failure 3 --- Euler rates are not body rates.} Found only after
the first two were fixed. The Euler-rate vector
$[\dot q_1,\dot q_2,\dot q_3]$ equals the body angular velocity $\bm{\omega}$
only at $\bm{q}=\bm{0}$; over the recovery trajectory the discrepancy reached
$73\,\%$. Correcting both the attitude and the rate feedback moved the
Simscape recovery from $3.33^\circ$ to $4.14^\circ$, at which point it
matched an independently written nonlinear simulator to $0.35\,\%$.

\medskip
\noindent
All three are the same mistake in different clothes: a signal correct at the
equilibrium was treated as correct everywhere. That is precisely the
assumption a balancing controller is not entitled to make, because the
controller only acts when the system is away from equilibrium.

\subsubsection{Reduced attitude}
\label{sec:reduced-attitude}

Let $\hat{\bm{\gamma}}$ be the unit vector along gravity expressed in the
cube body frame, and $\hat{\bm{g}}_B$ its value at the balanced attitude --- a
per-corner constant. Then
%
\begin{align}
  \hat{\bm{\gamma}} &= \mathrm{normalise}(-\bm{a}_{\mathrm{cal}})
    && \text{directly what the accelerometer gives}
    \label{eq:gam-meas}\\[0.2em]
  \dot{\hat{\bm{\gamma}}} &= -\,\bm{\omega}\times\hat{\bm{\gamma}}
    && \text{kinematics, one line, no chart}
    \label{eq:gam-kin}\\[0.2em]
  \bm{\phi} &= -\,\hat{\bm{g}}_B\times\hat{\bm{\gamma}}
    && \text{the entire attitude computation}
    \label{eq:phi}
\end{align}
%
Three numbers, one norm constraint, two degrees of freedom;
$\hat{\bm{\gamma}}=\hat{\bm{g}}_B$ is balanced.

The property that does the work is that $\bm{\phi}$ in \eqref{eq:phi} is
perpendicular to $\hat{\bm{g}}_B$ \emph{by construction} --- a cross product
with $\hat{\bm{g}}_B$ always is. Yaw therefore cannot enter the tilt channel.
Not approximately, not to first order: exactly, at any attitude, at any tilt
magnitude. This is the direct repair of Failure~2, where the projection had
to be applied as a correction valid only near equilibrium.

Each property was checked against the nonlinear model rather than assumed:

\subsubsection{The limits of this choice}

Stating them is part of the argument. The correction term
$\bm{e}=\hat{\bm{g}}\times\hat{\bm{\gamma}}$ has magnitude
$\sin(\text{error})$: well conditioned below $90^\circ$, but zero again at
$180^\circ$, so an inverted cube can lock onto the antipode. The mitigation
is a reset when $\hat{\bm{g}}\cdot\hat{\bm{\gamma}}<0$ for $N$ consecutive
cycles. Reduced attitude is also the right choice \emph{because} yaw is
irrelevant to corner balancing; jump-up and corner-to-corner locomotion,
where $\hat{\bm{g}}_B$ moves through $67$--$107^\circ$, require large-attitude
tracking against a full reference and a quaternion MEKF becomes the
appropriate structure. Finally, a magnetometer would not help: it would make
yaw observable while leaving it uncontrollable, and the degeneracy is
two-sided (\S\ref{sec:structural}).

\subsubsection{Loop rate}
\label{sec:loop-rate}

The outer loop runs at 400\,Hz. The figure is bounded from below by two
independent requirements and from above by the CAN-FD budget, and the
binding lower bound is not the one usually quoted.

\paragraph{Lower bound 1: discrete stability.} Discretising the open-loop
plant with a zero-order hold and closing the loop with the gain of
\S\ref{sec:lqr}, including the one-cycle computational delay between
sampling and actuation, the closed loop leaves the unit circle below
%
\begin{equation}
  f_{\min}^{\mathrm{stab}} = 38.3\ \mathrm{Hz},
  \label{eq:fmin-stab}
\end{equation}
%
which is $29\times$ the open-loop divergence rate
$\lambda/2\pi = 1.31$\,Hz. The margin above this floor is large but the floor
itself is not the design driver.

The delay term matters more than the sampling. Without it the discretised
loop remains stable down to well under 20\,Hz; with it the floor rises to
38.3\,Hz. Sample-and-actuate latency, not sample rate as such, is what
consumes phase.

\paragraph{Lower bound 2: anti-aliasing.} The accelerometer sees structural
and wheel-borne content well above the control bandwidth. Any of it above
$f/2$ folds down into the estimator passband, where it is
indistinguishable from tilt and cannot be removed by any subsequent
filtering. Requiring the folding frequency to sit clear of the sensor's
useful band gives
%
\begin{equation}
  f_{\min}^{\mathrm{alias}} = 281\ \mathrm{Hz},
  \label{eq:fmin-alias}
\end{equation}
%
which exceeds \eqref{eq:fmin-stab} by a factor of $7.3$ and is therefore the
binding constraint. \emph{The loop rate is set by the sensor, not by the
controller.} This is worth stating explicitly because the stability floor is
the quantity usually computed and it is, here, an order of magnitude too
permissive.

\paragraph{Upper bound: cycle budget and rate quantisation.} At 400\,Hz the
cycle budget is 2.5\,ms, which must accommodate an IMU read over SPI, three
encoder and torque exchanges over CAN-FD, and the control computation. The
control computation is negligible --- roughly 60 multiply--accumulates
(\S\ref{sec:architecture}) --- so the budget is dominated by bus traffic, and
this is the constraint that closes the interval from above.

Encoder quantisation contributes a second, weaker upper bound. Differencing a
16-bit absolute encoder gives a rate noise floor of
$\mathrm{LSB}\times f = 9.587\times10^{-5}\times400 = 0.038$\,rad\,s$^{-1}$,
$0.10\,\%$ of the wheel-speed cap, and it scales linearly with $f$. At
400\,Hz this is immaterial; it is recorded because it is the term that would
eventually penalise a faster loop.

\paragraph{What 400\,Hz buys.} Discretisation costs closed-loop damping, and
the cost is a smooth function of rate rather than a threshold:

\medskip
\noindent
400\,Hz therefore sits $1.4\times$ above the binding anti-aliasing bound,
$10\times$ above the stability floor, and comfortably inside the CAN-FD
budget, and it is verified against the discretised plant rather than assumed
from the continuous design. \emph{The return-difference identity
$M_s = 1$ of \S\ref{sec:validation} does not survive discretisation}, so the
discrete poles are checked directly at the design rate and are not inferred
from the continuous margin.

\subsection{Plant model and linearisation}
\label{sec:plant}

\subsubsection{Modelling assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

Four assumptions define the model. Each is stated with the reason it is
admissible and the circumstance under which it would fail.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Fixed point contact, no slip and no bounce.} The cube rotates
        about a stationary point at the balancing corner. The corner
        truncation on this build was restored to a point, so the contact is
        the exact geometric corner and no contact offset is required. Even
        the original $0.45$\,mm edge cut amounted to only $0.26$\,mm along
        the diagonal --- an angular contribution of $0.081^\circ$, below the
        estimator noise floor. The assumption fails at jump-up, where contact
        is impulsive and intermittent.

  \item \textbf{Rigid bodies throughout.} Frame and wheels are rigid, with no
        structural compliance. Admissible because the lowest structural mode
        is well above the control bandwidth of \S\ref{sec:loop-rate}; it is
        the assumption that would be challenged first if the printed frame
        were made lighter.

  \item \textbf{Wheels axisymmetric about their spin axes.} This is stronger
        than it appears: it makes $\bm{\Theta}$ \emph{body-constant}. The
        inertia tensor does not depend on wheel \emph{angle}, only on wheel
        \emph{rate} through the momentum term. Without it the plant would be
        time-varying in the wheel angles and no constant-gain design would be
        available.

  \item \textbf{Pivot friction neglected in the design plant.} Retained in
        the nonlinear model of \S\ref{sec:nonlinearities} but dropped from the
        linearisation, where it is second order about the equilibrium. The
        coefficient is a placeholder pending a breakaway test.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Nonlinear equations of motion}
\label{sec:nonlinear-eom}

Let $O$ be the contact corner, $\bm{\omega}$ the body angular velocity about
$O$, $\bm{\rho}$ the three wheel rates \emph{relative to the body},
$\bm{A}_w$ the matrix whose columns are the wheel spin axes, $I_s$ the wheel
inertia about its own spin axis, $\bm{\Theta}$ the inertia of the whole
assembly about $O$ with all bodies rigid, $\bm{r}_c$ the vector from $O$ to
the centre of mass, and $\hat{\bm{\gamma}}$ the gravity direction in body
coordinates (\S\ref{sec:reduced-attitude}).

The angular momentum about $O$, expressed in body axes, is
%
\begin{equation}
  \bm{L} = \bm{\Theta}\bm{\omega} + \bm{A}_w I_s \bm{\rho}.
  \label{eq:momentum}
\end{equation}
%
The first term is the momentum the assembly would have if the wheels were
locked; the second accounts for their relative spin. Euler's equation in the
rotating body frame gives
%
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\bm{L}} + \bm{\omega}\times\bm{L} = \bm{M}_{\mathrm{ext}},
  \qquad
  \bm{M}_{\mathrm{ext}} = m g\,(\bm{r}_c\times\hat{\bm{\gamma}})
                          + \bm{\tau}_{\mathrm{pivot}}.
  \label{eq:euler}
\end{equation}
%
Each wheel obeys
%
\begin{equation}
  I_s\!\left(\hat{\bm{a}}_k\cdot\dot{\bm{\omega}} + \dot\rho_k\right) = u_k,
  \label{eq:wheel}
\end{equation}
%
where $u_k$ is the motor torque applied to wheel $k$. Solving \eqref{eq:wheel}
for $I_s\dot{\bm{\rho}}$ and substituting into the time derivative of
\eqref{eq:momentum} eliminates $\dot{\bm{\rho}}$ from the body equation:
%
\begin{align}
  \bm{\Theta}\dot{\bm{\omega}}
    + \bm{A}_w\bm{u} - \bm{A}_w I_s \bm{A}_w^{\top}\dot{\bm{\omega}}
    + \bm{\omega}\times\bm{L}
  &= \bm{M}_{\mathrm{ext}},
\end{align}
%
and defining the \emph{reduced inertia}
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\bm{\Theta}} \;=\; \bm{\Theta} - \bm{A}_w I_s \bm{A}_w^{\top}
  \label{eq:Tbar}
\end{equation}
%
the equations of motion close as
%
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{align}
  \bar{\bm{\Theta}}\,\dot{\bm{\omega}}
    &= m g\,(\bm{r}_c\times\hat{\bm{\gamma}}) \;-\; \bm{u}
       \;-\; \bm{\omega}\times\bm{L}
       \;-\; \bm{\tau}_{\mathrm{pivot}}
       \label{eq:eom-body}\\[0.25em]
  I_s\dot{\bm{\rho}} &= \bm{u} - I_s\bm{A}_w^{\top}\dot{\bm{\omega}}
       \label{eq:eom-wheel}\\[0.25em]
  \dot{\hat{\bm{\gamma}}} &= -\,\bm{\omega}\times\hat{\bm{\gamma}}
       \label{eq:eom-gamma}
\end{empheq}

Three features of \eqref{eq:eom-body}--\eqref{eq:eom-gamma} govern everything
that follows.

\paragraph{Momentum Exchange.} The wheels
exchange momentum with the body; they do not generate it. Torque does not
push against the world, and $\bm{L}$ is conserved under $\bm{u}$ alone. This
is the origin of the momentum-limited envelope
(\S\ref{sec:nonlinearities}).

\paragraph{Reduced Inertia.} The wheel inertia about
its own axis is subtracted because that inertia is already accounted for in
\eqref{eq:eom-wheel}; leaving it in $\bm{\Theta}$ would double-count it. On
this build $\bar{\bm{\Theta}}$ differs from $\bm{\Theta}$ by
$4.87\times10^{-4}$ on each diagonal entry, about $2.4\,\%$ --- small, but it
shifts $\lambda$ by $1.2\,\%$ and it is not optional.

\paragraph{Gyroscopic Term.} $\bm{\omega}\times
\bm{L}$ is quadratic and vanishes at the equilibrium, so it plays no part in
the gain synthesis. It is retained in the nonlinear simulator used for the
envelope work, and it is the reason the envelope results of
\S\ref{sec:results} are simulated rather than computed in closed form.

\subsubsection{The state vector}
\label{sec:state-vector}

Throughout the remainder of this chapter the state is
%
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \bm{x} = \begin{bmatrix}\bm{\phi}\\[0.15em] \bm{\omega}\\[0.15em]
                          \bm{\rho}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{9},
  \qquad
  \bm{\phi} = -\,\hat{\bm{g}}_B\times\hat{\bm{\gamma}}
  \;}
  \label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
%
--- reduced attitude, body rate, wheel rate, each in body axes $X,Y,Z$ in
that order. $\bm{\phi}$ and $\bm{\omega}$ come from the estimator of
\S\ref{sec:estimation}; $\bm{\rho}$ comes directly from the encoders and
needs no estimation.

The interpretation of $\bm{\phi}$ deserves a line, because it is what makes
the projector $\bm{P}$ appear later. For a small rotation $\delta\bm{\theta}$
away from the balanced attitude,
$\hat{\bm{\gamma}} \approx \hat{\bm{g}}_B - \delta\bm{\theta}\times
\hat{\bm{g}}_B$, and therefore
%
\begin{align}
  \bm{\phi}
   &= -\hat{\bm{g}}_B\times
      \left(\hat{\bm{g}}_B - \delta\bm{\theta}\times\hat{\bm{g}}_B\right)
    = \hat{\bm{g}}_B\times(\delta\bm{\theta}\times\hat{\bm{g}}_B)
    \notag\\
   &= \delta\bm{\theta}\,(\hat{\bm{g}}_B\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{g}}_B)
      - \hat{\bm{g}}_B\,(\hat{\bm{g}}_B\!\cdot\!\delta\bm{\theta})
    = \bm{P}\,\delta\bm{\theta},
  \qquad
  \bm{P} = \bm{I} - \hat{\bm{g}}_B\hat{\bm{g}}_B^{\top}.
  \label{eq:phi-is-projected-rotation}
\end{align}
%
\emph{$\bm{\phi}$ is the rotation vector with its vertical component removed.}
It is not an approximation of the attitude error; it is exactly the part of
the attitude error that gravity can act on and that an accelerometer can see.

\subsubsection{Linearisation}
\label{sec:linearisation}

\paragraph{Gravitational Term.} Because
$\bm{r}_c = -\ell\,\hat{\bm{g}}_B$ is body-fixed,
%
\begin{equation}
  m g\,(\bm{r}_c\times\hat{\bm{\gamma}})
  = -m g \ell\,(\hat{\bm{g}}_B\times\hat{\bm{\gamma}})
  = S_g\,\bm{\phi},
  \qquad S_g = m g \ell,
  \label{eq:grav-exact}
\end{equation}
%
with no small-angle step anywhere. Substituting
\eqref{eq:phi-is-projected-rotation} gives the form used in the design plant,
$S_g\bm{P}\,\delta\bm{\theta}$, and since $\bm{P}$ is idempotent and
$\bm{\phi}$ already lies in its range, $S_g\bm{\phi} = S_g\bm{P}\bm{\phi}$.

This identity is the reason the linear plant used for gain synthesis and the
nonlinear plant used for envelope work can be compared at all. It was checked
numerically rather than assumed: the two agree to
$9.9\times10^{-17}$\,N\,m (\S\ref{sec:reduced-attitude}). The controller is
not approximating the plant's attitude behaviour --- the two are the same
object.

\paragraph{The design plant.} Dropping the gyroscopic and pivot-friction
terms, which are second order about the equilibrium, and taking
$\bm{A}_w = \bm{I}_3$ since the wheel axes are the cube axes:
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\bm{\Theta}}\,\dot{\bm{\omega}} = S_g\bm{P}\bm{\phi} - \bm{u},
  \qquad
  I_s\dot{\bm{\rho}} = \bm{u} - I_s\dot{\bm{\omega}}.
\end{equation}
%
In the ordering \eqref{eq:state} this is $\dot{\bm{x}} = \bm{A}\bm{x} +
\bm{B}\bm{u}$ with
%
\begin{equation}
  \bm{A} = \begin{bmatrix}
      \bm{0} & \bm{I} & \bm{0}\\
      \bm{G} & \bm{0} & \bm{0}\\
     -\bm{G} & \bm{0} & \bm{0}
    \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \bm{B} = \begin{bmatrix}
      \bm{0}\\
      -\bar{\bm{\Theta}}^{-1}\\
      I_s^{-1}\bm{I} + \bar{\bm{\Theta}}^{-1}
    \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \bm{G} = \bar{\bm{\Theta}}^{-1} S_g \bm{P}.
  \label{eq:AB}
\end{equation}

Three structural remarks.

\emph{First}, the third block row is $-\bm{G}$, an exact copy of the second
with the sign reversed. That is momentum exchange written in state-space
form: whatever angular acceleration gravity imparts to the body appears with
opposite sign on the wheels.

\emph{Second}, the first block row is $\dot{\bm{\phi}} = \bm{\omega}$, not
$\bm{P}\bm{\omega}$. The component of $\bm{\phi}$ along $\hat{\bm{g}}_B$
therefore accumulates with yaw rate. It is harmless, because $\bm{G}$
annihilates it --- $\bm{P}\hat{\bm{g}}_B = \bm{0}$ exactly --- so it never
feeds back into the dynamics. It is nevertheless removed from the control law
by the projection of \S\ref{sec:structural}, because a state that drifts
without bound should not multiply a gain.

\emph{Third}, $\bm{P}$ has rank~2. That single fact generates both structural
degeneracies of \S\ref{sec:structural} and is the reason the Riccati problem
must be posed on eight states rather than nine.

\subsubsection{From one degree of freedom to three}
\label{sec:panel-to-cube}

The Stage-1 planar panel is the same plant with every matrix collapsed to a
scalar, and the correspondence is close enough to be useful as a check.

\paragraph{What generalises.} The block structure of \eqref{eq:AB} survives
intact with scalars promoted to matrices; the unstable-pole relation
$\lambda = \sqrt{S_g/\bar\Theta}$ survives with $\bar{\bm{\Theta}}$ read on
the tilt plane; the wheel remains a pure integrator, so a wheel-rate gain
remains necessary; and the Bryson weights were carried from panel to cube
\emph{unchanged}. That last was deliberate and is a debugging strategy rather
than an economy --- same physics, same weights, so any surprise in the cube
result is located in the plant and not in the tuning.

\paragraph{What breaks.} Four things, in descending order of consequence.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Gravity acquires a projector.} The scalar $S_g$ becomes
        $S_g\bm{P}$ of rank~2, and with it come the two structural
        degeneracies of \S\ref{sec:structural}. There is no
        one-dimensional analogue; it must be handled by an exact state-space
        reduction, not by tuning.
  \item \textbf{Attitude stops being a number}, with the consequences set out
        in \S\ref{sec:attitude-representation}.
  \item \textbf{The wheel axes acquire an offset} that the obvious model does
        not have.
  \item \textbf{One accelerometer error term stops cancelling.} The
        centripetal term is quadratic in $\bm{\omega}$ and always directed
        inward, so unlike the tangential term it has a DC component and does
        not average away over a completed recovery
        (\S\ref{sec:estimation}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{And the envelope shrinks.} The panel recovers from roughly
$11.5^\circ$, the cube from roughly $3.9^\circ$. The cube is the larger and
more capable machine and has the smaller envelope, which invites the question
if left unaddressed. Taking the momentum-limited bound

$1-\cos\theta_{\max}$ for a release from rest:

\subsection{Structural properties: what cannot be controlled and what cannot be seen}
\label{sec:structural}

The plant of \eqref{eq:AB} has nine states, but the control problem is
eight-dimensional. One rotational degree of freedom --- rotation about the
vertical --- is degenerate, and it is degenerate at \emph{both} ends: it
cannot be driven, and it cannot be observed. The two facts have different
causes and different consequences, and both must be handled explicitly.

This is not a numerical near-degeneracy to be regularised away. Both
statements hold exactly, to machine precision, and both follow from a single
line of the model.

\subsubsection{Yaw momentum is conserved: the uncontrollable direction}
\label{sec:uncontrollable}

\paragraph{Derivation.} Let $\bm{L} = \bm{\Theta}\bm{\omega} +
I_s\bm{\rho}$ be the angular momentum about the contact point. Its component
along the vertical evolves as
%
\begin{align}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\!\left(\hat{\bm{g}}_B\!\cdot\!\bm{L}\right)
  &= \hat{\bm{g}}_B^{\top}
     \left(\bm{\Theta}\dot{\bm{\omega}} + I_s\dot{\bm{\rho}}\right)
   = \hat{\bm{g}}_B^{\top}
     \left(\bm{\Theta}\dot{\bm{\omega}} + \bm{u} - I_s\dot{\bm{\omega}}\right)
   \notag\\
  &= \hat{\bm{g}}_B^{\top}
     \left(\bar{\bm{\Theta}}\dot{\bm{\omega}} + \bm{u}\right)
   = \hat{\bm{g}}_B^{\top}
     \left(S_g\bm{P}\bm{\phi} - \bm{u} + \bm{u}\right)
   = S_g\,\hat{\bm{g}}_B^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{\phi}
   = 0,
  \label{eq:momentum-conservation}
\end{align}
%
because $\bm{P}\hat{\bm{g}}_B = \bm{0}$ by construction. The control $\bm{u}$
cancels identically between the body and wheel equations --- which is exactly
the statement that the wheels are internal. Verified numerically on random
states and inputs: $\hat{\bm{g}}_B^{\top}(\bm{\Theta}\dot{\bm{\omega}} +
I_s\dot{\bm{\rho}}) = 6.7\times10^{-16}$, and
$\hat{\bm{g}}_B^{\top}S_g\bm{P} = 3.4\times10^{-16}$.

Physically: gravity produces no moment about the vertical, and the wheels can
only exchange momentum with the body, not with the world. Nothing in the
plant can change $\hat{\bm{g}}_B\!\cdot\!\bm{L}$. A yaw kick therefore
persists indefinitely in the frictionless model. On hardware, contact
friction at the corner supplies an external yaw torque and it bleeds away, so
the model is the pessimistic case.

\paragraph{The numerical evidence.} The controllability matrix has

& $1.31\!\times\!10^{-6}$ & $1.85\!\times\!10^{-7}$
& $\bm{9.19\!\times\!10^{-17}}$\\

\end{tabular}
\caption{Normalised singular values of $\mathcal{C}(\bm{A},\bm{B})$. The gap

\noindent
so $\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{C} = 8$ of $9$. The left singular vector of the
vanishing direction aligns with the predicted conserved quantity to
%
\begin{equation}
  \left|\cos\angle\!\left(\bm{v}_{\mathrm{unc}},\,
  \begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}\\ \bm{\Theta}\hat{\bm{g}}_B\\ I_s\hat{\bm{g}}_B

\end{equation}
%
which is what converts a rank deficiency into a physical statement. A rank

$\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{B} = 3.3\times10^{-13}$.

\paragraph{Stabilisability.} The system is \emph{stabilisable}, not
controllable, and stabilisability is sufficient here for a specific reason:
the uncontrollable mode sits at exactly $s = 0$. It is marginally stable, it
does not grow, and balancing requires only the two tilt directions. An
uncontrollable mode in the right half-plane would end the project; one at the
origin is a bookkeeping problem.

\subsubsection{Yaw angle is unobservable: the second degeneracy}
\label{sec:unobservable}

An accelerometer measures the direction of gravity. One vector measurement
determines two rotational degrees of freedom, not three: it tells you which
way is down, and it never tells you which way you are facing.

With the full state available, $\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{O} = 9$ of $9$. With
the reduced-attitude measurement actually available on this machine,
$\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{O} = 8$ of $9$, and the unobservable direction is

%

\emph{angle}, and nothing else. Yaw \emph{rate} is fully observable: the
gyroscope measures it directly. It is the integral that cannot be recovered.

\subsubsection{Exact Eight-state Reduction}
\label{sec:reduction}

Let $\bm{v} = [\bm{0};\ \bm{\Theta}\hat{\bm{g}}_B;\ I_s\hat{\bm{g}}_B]$ and
let $\bm{V}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{9\times8}$ be an orthonormal basis for
$\bm{v}^{\perp}$. The design is performed on
%
\begin{equation}
  \bm{A}_r = \bm{V}_2^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{V}_2, \quad
  \bm{B}_r = \bm{V}_2^{\top}\bm{B}, \quad
  \bm{Q}_r = \bm{V}_2^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{V}_2,
  \qquad
  \bm{K} = \bm{K}_r\bm{V}_2^{\top}.
  \label{eq:reduction}
\end{equation}
%
The reduced pair is fully controllable, $\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{C}(\bm{A}_r,
\bm{B}_r) = 8$ of $8$, and the resulting gain places no authority on the
conserved direction: $\lVert\bm{K}\bm{v}\rVert/\lVert\bm{v}\rVert =
5.5\times10^{-16}$.

The Hamiltonian row is the diagnostic. A well-posed algebraic Riccati problem
has no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis; each one is a mode the stable and
anti-stable invariant subspaces cannot separate. At nine states there are two
--- the conserved direction and its adjoint. At eight there are none.

\paragraph{Silent Failure in LQR.} \texttt{lqr} returns a gain matrix
without warning, and that gain places $\lVert\bm{K}\bm{v}\rVert = 0.168$ on a
direction the actuators cannot move. The controller then reads a real,
drifting, measurable quantity --- accumulated yaw momentum --- and commands
torque in response to it. That torque cannot change the quantity it is
responding to, so its entire effect is a disturbance to the two directions
that \emph{do} respond. Authority is spent to no purpose, and the loss is
invisible in any check that only asks whether the closed loop is stable.

\emph{A design that runs without error is not the same as a design that is
well posed.} This is the clearest instance in the project of a check
mattering more than a result.

\subsubsection{The yaw-angle projection}
\label{sec:projection}

The reduction of \eqref{eq:reduction} removes the uncontrollable direction.
It does not remove the unobservable one, because the two are different
directions --- compare the rows of \cref{tab:degeneracies}. A second,
separate step is required.

Recall from \S\ref{sec:linearisation} that the first block row of $\bm{A}$ is
$\dot{\bm{\phi}} = \bm{\omega}$ rather than $\bm{P}\bm{\omega}$, so the
component of $\bm{\phi}$ along $\hat{\bm{g}}_B$ accumulates with yaw rate
without bound. It is harmless to the \emph{dynamics}, because $\bm{G}$
annihilates it. It is not harmless in the \emph{control law}, because the
reduced gain places $\lVert\bm{K}[\hat{\bm{g}}_B;\bm{0};\bm{0}]\rVert =
0.346$ on it --- and that component of the estimate is not a measurement of
anything. It is integrated gyro drift.

The remedy is to project it out of the gain itself:
%
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\bm{K}_p = \bm{K}\,\mathrm{blkdiag}(\bm{P},\bm{I},\bm{I})\;}
  \label{eq:Kp}
\end{equation}
%
after which the gain on that direction is $4.97\times10^{-16}$. \textbf{Use
$\bm{K}_p$ in firmware, not $\bm{K}$.}

\paragraph{Marginal mode Influence.}

The second column is the part worth pausing on. Under $\bm{K}$ the yaw angle
is driven to zero at $4.26\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; under $\bm{K}_p$ it is left to
drift. Removing that control action makes the \emph{slowest controlled mode
faster}, from $-4.26$ to $-7.82\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ --- a factor of $1.8$. The
projection is not a cosmetic tidying of a harmless term: authority previously
spent chasing an unobservable state is returned to the states that matter,
and the loop is measurably better for it.

\subsubsection{A tolerance trap in the rank test}
\label{sec:rank-trap}

The entire argument above rests on a rank count, so it is worth recording how
that count can be got wrong.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Call} & \textbf{Result}\\
\midrule
\texttt{rank(Co)} \quad (default, relative tolerance) & $\bm{8}$ --- correct\\
\texttt{rank(Co, 1e-9)} \quad (absolute tolerance) & $9$ --- \textbf{wrong}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
$\lVert\mathcal{C}\rVert = 1.105\times10^{9}$, so an absolute tolerance of
$10^{-9}$ is $9\times10^{-19}$ of the matrix scale --- far below the double
precision floor, and therefore no threshold at all. Every singular value,
including the one at $9.19\times10^{-17}$ that \emph{is} numerically zero,
counts as nonzero.

The failure is silent and directional: it reports the system as fully
controllable, which is the answer that requires no further work. Had it been
believed, the reduction of \S\ref{sec:reduction} would never have been
written, \texttt{lqr} would have returned a gain without complaint, and the
first symptom would have appeared on hardware as authority quietly leaking
into a direction that does not move.

\emph{A tolerance must be chosen relative to the scale of the matrix it is
applied to.} MATLAB's default does this; a hand-supplied absolute figure
carried over from another problem does not.

\subsection{State estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}

\subsubsection{The accelerometer measurement problem}
\label{sec:why-estimator}

An accelerometer measures specific force, not gravity: the difference between
the acceleration of its case and free fall. Stationary, the case is supported
at $1\,g$ and the reading corresponds to the gravity direction. Under any
other acceleration it does not.

For a body rotating about a fixed pivot with the sensor at distance $d$, the
case experiences a tangential acceleration $\ddot\theta\,d$ and a centripetal
acceleration $\dot\theta^2 d$, giving in body axes
%
\begin{equation}
  f_x = \ddot\theta\,d + g\sin\theta,
  \qquad
  f_y = g\cos\theta - \dot\theta^2 d,
  \label{eq:specific-force}
\end{equation}
%
so the direct readout $\hat\theta = \mathrm{atan2}(f_x, f_y)$ is corrupted by
both terms.

The corruption is correlated with the manoeuvre rather than random. At rest
$\ddot\theta = 0$ and the measurement is exact; at maximum control effort
$\ddot\theta$ peaks and the error is largest. \Cref{tab:lever-arm} gives the
magnitude for this cube with the IMU at the geometric centre, $d = 130$\,mm
from any corner.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity to standing tilt error}
\label{sec:bias-sensitivity}

The lever-arm term is transient. A second requirement, arising from constant
estimate error, binds more tightly on this machine.

A constant tilt-estimate error $e$ causes the controller to drive the estimate
to zero, so the cube balances $e$ from true vertical. Gravity then applies a
constant moment which the wheels absorb indefinitely, and the steady state
gives a standing wheel speed
%
\begin{equation}
  \dot\varphi_{ss} = \frac{|K_1|}{|K_3|}\,e,
  \qquad \frac{|K_1|}{|K_3|} = 4365 ,
  \label{eq:standing-wheel}
\end{equation}
%
against $635$ for the planar panel. The cube is $6.9\times$ more sensitive to

\subsubsection{Selected structure}
\label{sec:chosen}

An explicit complementary filter with PI correction, applied directly to the
gravity direction. The filter estimates $\hat{\bm{g}}$, the gravity direction
in body coordinates, and a gyro bias $\hat{\bm{b}}$:
%
\begin{align}
  \bm{e} &= \hat{\bm{g}}\times\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{meas}}
    \label{eq:cf-error}\\
  \dot{\hat{\bm{g}}} &= -\left(\bm{\omega}-\hat{\bm{b}}\right)\times\hat{\bm{g}}
     \;+\; k_P\,\bm{e}\times\hat{\bm{g}}
    \label{eq:cf-prop}\\
  \dot{\hat{\bm{b}}} &= +\,k_I\,\bm{e}
    \label{eq:cf-bias}
\end{align}
%
with $\hat{\bm{g}}$ renormalised each cycle. The controller state follows as
$\bm{\phi} = -\hat{\bm{g}}_B\times\hat{\bm{g}}$
(\S\ref{sec:reduced-attitude}).

Equation \eqref{eq:cf-prop} combines two sensors over complementary frequency
bands. The gyro term is accurate over short intervals and drifts over long
ones; the accelerometer term has no drift but carries the lever-arm error of
\eqref{eq:specific-force} at high frequency. The gain $k_P$ sets the
crossover at $\tau = 1/k_P$.

\paragraph{Bias estimation.} A proportional-only filter leaves a steady-state
tilt offset $e = b/k_P$ proportional to gyro bias. Propagated through
\eqref{eq:standing-wheel}:

An uncalibrated consumer MEMS gyro typically exhibits
$1$--$2^\circ$\,s$^{-1}$ of zero-rate offset, consuming approximately half
the momentum budget at rest. Startup calibration is therefore required, and
the integral term \eqref{eq:cf-bias} removes the residual, including thermal
drift of order $0.1$--$0.3^\circ$\,s$^{-1}$ over a ten-minute session for a
sensor mounted near three motors without airflow.

The sign in \eqref{eq:cf-bias} is $+k_I\bm{e}$, established by measurement:
the opposite sign converges smoothly to five times the true bias.

\paragraph{Yaw-rate bias observability.} The component of gyro bias along
$\hat{\bm{g}}_B$ is unobservable, gravity providing no information about
rotation about gravity (\S\ref{sec:unobservable}). Against a true bias of
$0.149^\circ$\,s$^{-1}$ in that direction the estimator returns exactly
$0.000$, while both perpendicular components converge to truth. This is the
correct response to an unconstrained direction. A quaternion MEKF represents
that direction explicitly and its covariance grows without bound unless
constrained by hand \cite{markley2003attitude}; the reduced-attitude form
does not admit the state.

\paragraph{Gain selection.} $k_P = 4$, $k_I = 0.5$. The response to $k_I$ is
flat across $0.1$--$2.0$. The response to $k_P$ is not flat and is bounded
above, as set out in \S\ref{sec:kp-edge}.

\FLAG{$k_P$: the firmware note and the estimator specification give $4$; the
cube envelope sweep gives $5$ as the optimum ($2.75^\circ$ recovery against
$2.49$--$2.58^\circ$ at $k_P = 2$), with a hard edge above. If $5$ is
current, this paragraph and \S\ref{sec:kp-edge} both change, and $4$ should
be presented as deliberate headroom rather than as the optimum.}

\subsubsection{Rejection of the lever-arm term}
\label{sec:cancellation}

Two mechanisms act on the tangential error term, and their combined effect
exceeds what either provides alone.

The first is attenuation. The lever-arm error is a transient of duration
${\sim}0.2$\,s and the accelerometer path is low-passed at $\tau = 1/k_P$, so
most of the transient does not reach the estimate.

The second is cancellation. The error term is $\ddot\theta\,d$ and
low-pass filtering approximates integration, but
$\int\ddot\theta\,\mathrm{d}t = \Delta\dot\theta$, which is zero across a
completed recovery: the rig is at rest before and after. The sign of the
error reverses between the accelerating and decelerating phases, and the two
halves cancel.

Measured on the panel, a $25.9^\circ$ raw peak becomes a $0.29^\circ$
filtered error, a reduction of $300\times$. The residual is not lever arm but
the gyro-bias floor $b\tau = 0.005\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}} \times 1.0\
\mathrm{s} = 0.286^\circ$, which matches the observed value to two decimals
at every mounting distance.

\paragraph{Excluded correction terms.} An explicit
$\dot{\bm{\omega}}\times\bm{r}$ correction is not applied. It requires a
differentiated gyro signal and the filter already rejects the term it would
remove, so it would contribute noise without benefit.

\paragraph{Accelerometer rejection gate.} The gate discarding samples whose
magnitude departs from $1\,g$ is set at $10$--$20\,\%$. A tighter gate fires
during the acceleration phase but not the deceleration phase, which removes
one half of the cancelling pair and leaves the other. The width is chosen to
pass both halves of the transient while still rejecting an impact.

Below the optimum, phase lag dominates. Above it, sufficient lever-arm error

tilts the estimate, the controller commands torque, the torque produces

\S\ref{sec:1d-to-3d}. Removing that lag requires a model-based estimator.

\subsubsection{Calibration requirements}
\label{sec:calibration}

\paragraph{Startup gyro-bias calibration.} Three seconds stationary at boot,
averaged and subtracted from subsequent samples. Required by
\cref{tab:gyro-bias}.

\paragraph{Twelve-parameter accelerometer calibration.} Three biases, three
scale factors and six cross-axis terms, obtained from a set of static
orientations. Normalising $\hat{\bm{\gamma}}$ to unit length removes
common-mode scale error but not differential scale error between axes. In one
plane a differential error appears as a gain change and is largely absorbed;
in three dimensions it appears as a direction-dependent tilt bias, against
the $0.105^\circ$ total budget of \cref{tab:bias-budget}.

\paragraph{Mounting rotation.} $\bm{R}_{IB}$, the rotation from IMU axes to
cube body axes, is verified by a six-face test rather than taken from the
datasheet drawing. A mounting misalignment produces a constant tilt offset
and enters \cref{tab:bias-budget} identically to gyro bias.

\subsubsection{Standing wheel speed as a diagnostic}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

A non-zero standing wheel speed has two possible causes, gyro bias or a
centre-of-mass offset, and they are separable without instrumentation:
rotating the cube $180^\circ$ about the vertical and repeating the run
reverses the sign of a COM offset and leaves a gyro bias unchanged. The test
distinguishes recalibration from shimming.

By \cref{tab:bias-budget}, standing wheel speed is the most informative
single health indicator available and is logged on every run.

\subsubsection{Upgrade path}
\label{sec:upgrade-path}

The complementary filter is sufficient for corner balancing and is the
implemented configuration. The $0.67^\circ$ of envelope identified in
\S\ref{sec:1d-to-3d} is recoverable through a four-stage progression.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Explicit complementary filter with PI bias correction. Implemented.
  \item Steady-state linear Kalman filter on
        $[\bm{\phi};\,\bm{\omega};\,\bm{b}_{\mathrm{gyro}}]$. The standard
        objection to a linear Kalman filter for attitude --- that any
        parameterisation of $SO(3)$ carries a singularity --- does not apply,
        because $\bm{\phi}$ is not a parameterisation of $SO(3)$ but a vector
        in the tangent plane at the balanced attitude, already linear in the
        plant (\S\ref{sec:linearisation}). Possessing the model, the filter
        reconstructs the lever-arm term analytically rather than rejecting
        it, removing the phase lag at source. The runtime cost is a constant gain
        matrix, identical to the complementary filter, and the separation
 principle allows composition with $\bm{K}_p$ unchanged. This stage
        accounts for the outstanding $0.67^\circ$.
  \item Augmentation with a disturbance-torque state, providing integral
        action against COM offset and constant friction without additional
        structure.
  \item MEKF. Deferred: it provides explicit bias states in place of
        \eqref{eq:cf-bias}, at the cost of six additional states and a
        covariance to tune. It becomes the appropriate structure at Stage~3,
        where large-attitude tracking against a full reference is required
        and the reduced-attitude formulation no longer applies
        (\S\ref{sec:attitude-representation}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Control design}
\label{sec:lqr}

\subsubsection{Choice of method}
\label{sec:why-lqr}

The plant of \eqref{eq:AB} is linear, time-invariant after the reduction of
\S\ref{sec:reduction}, fully actuated in the two tilt directions, and
equipped with a full state estimate. Under these conditions linear quadratic
regulation is the natural formulation, for three reasons specific to this
machine.

\paragraph{Multivariable coupling.} The two unstable tilt directions are not
aligned with any wheel axis, so each wheel contributes to both. Three
independent single-axis loops would require the coupling terms to be tuned by
hand against each other. LQR produces the coupled gain matrix directly, and
the resulting structure is visible in $\bm{K}_p$ below: negative diagonal
terms opposing each wheel's own tilt component, positive off-diagonal terms
assisting the other two.

\paragraph{Momentum regulation as a stated objective.} The wheel-rate state
enters the cost function on equal footing with attitude. Wheel speed is not a
secondary quantity to be limited after the fact; it is the constraint that
determines whether the machine balances indefinitely or for several seconds
(\S\ref{sec:bryson}).

\paragraph{Verifiable correctness.} Full-state LQR satisfies the
return-difference identity of Kalman \cite{kalman1964optimal}, which forces
$M_s = 1$ exactly. This provides an independent check on the implementation
that no manually tuned design offers (\S\ref{sec:validation}).

\subsubsection{Weight selection}
\label{sec:bryson}

Weights are assigned by Bryson's rule \cite{bryson1969applied}: each state is
weighted by the inverse square of its maximum acceptable excursion,
%
\begin{equation}
  \bm{Q} = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(
    \tfrac{1}{\theta_{\max}^2}\bm{I}_3,\;
    \tfrac{1}{\dot\theta_{\max}^2}\bm{I}_3,\;
    \tfrac{1}{\omega_{\mathrm{des}}^2}\bm{I}_3 \right),
  \qquad
  \bm{R} = \frac{\rho}{\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}\,\bm{I}_3 .
  \label{eq:bryson}
\end{equation}

The advantage of this parameterisation is that every entry is a physical
commitment rather than a tuning constant, and each can be checked against an
independently computed quantity.

Three of the four entries carry margin above the physical quantity, which is

\paragraph{Wheel-speed weight.} The entry $\omega_{\mathrm{des}}$ requires

outcome. A recovery from the full $3.88^\circ$ envelope requires
%
\begin{equation}
  h = \sqrt{2\,\bar\Theta\,S_g\,(1-\cos\theta)} = 0.0155\ \mathrm{N\,m\,s}
    = 31.8\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}} \text{ of wheel speed},
\end{equation}

which is $80\,\%$ of the available cap. There is no margin to spend on
standing wheel speed, so $\omega_{\mathrm{des}}$ is set to
$0.25\,\omega_{\mathrm{cap}}$ rather than to the motor's free-run speed. The
consequence of the alternative is quantified in \S\ref{sec:weight-sensitivity}.

\paragraph{Torque scale and control cost.} Only the ratio $\rho/\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}^2$
enters $\bm{R}$, so $\rho$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}$ are not independent
parameters. The effective control weight is $R = 208.3$.

\paragraph{Weight transfer from Stage 1.} The weights were carried unchanged
from the planar panel to the cube. Retaining them isolates the source of any
discrepancy between predicted and measured cube behaviour in the plant model
rather than in the tuning.

\subsubsection{Gain matrix}
\label{sec:gains}

Solving the reduced Riccati problem of \eqref{eq:reduction} and applying the
projection \eqref{eq:Kp}:

\left[\begin{array}{rrr|rrr|rrr}

  3.5194 & -6.8296 &  3.5136 &  0.4121 & -0.8481 &  0.4117 &  0.003826 & -0.003165 &  0.003816\\

with blocks acting on $\bm{\phi}$, $\bm{\omega}$ and $\bm{\rho}$

Three properties of \eqref{eq:Kp-numeric} serve as structural checks.

\paragraph{Sign pattern.} Attitude and rate blocks have negative diagonal and
positive off-diagonal entries: each wheel opposes its own tilt component and
assists the other two. A row deviating from this pattern indicates an error
in the contact point or in the gravity direction.

\paragraph{Diagonal uniformity.} The attitude diagonal is
$6.9157$, $6.8296$, $6.9237$, a spread of $1.4\,\%$, consistent with a mass
distribution near-isotropic about the balancing diagonal. A spread beyond
approximately $30\,\%$ indicates that the plant is not balancing on a corner.

\paragraph{Wheel-rate block.} The entries are three orders of magnitude below
the attitude entries. This is the ratio that governs bias sensitivity
(\S\ref{sec:bias-sensitivity}): $|K_1|/|K_3| = 4365$, giving a standing tilt
tolerance of $0.105^\circ$ for one fifth of the momentum budget.

\subsubsection{Firmware interface}
\label{sec:firmware-interface}

The control law reduces to one matrix--vector product per cycle:
%
\begin{equation}
  \bm{u}_{\mathrm{LQR}} = -\bm{K}_p
  \begin{bmatrix}\bm{\phi}\\ \bm{\omega}\\ \bm{\rho}\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \bm{\phi} = -\hat{\bm{g}}_B\times\hat{\bm{g}} .
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item State order is $[\bm{\phi}(3);\,\bm{\omega}(3);\,\bm{\rho}(3)]$ in
        body axes $X$, $Y$, $Z$.
  \item $\bm{\phi}$ and $\bm{\omega}$ are expressed in the cube body frame.
        IMU output is rotated by $\bm{R}_{IB}$, verified by a six-face test.
  \item $\rho_k > 0$ denotes rotation about the positive body axis $k$,
        verified per wheel on the bench.
  \item $\bm{K}_p$ is used, not $\bm{K}$ (\S\ref{sec:projection}).
  \item $\bm{K}_p$ is a per-corner constant. Changing balancing corner
        requires regenerating $\hat{\bm{g}}_B$, $\bm{P}$ and $\bm{K}_p$; no
        structural change is required.
  \item The output of this section is $\bm{u}_{\mathrm{LQR}}$, not the
        commanded torque. Friction feedforward, torque clamping and
        wheel-speed capping are applied afterwards
        (\S\ref{sec:nonlinearities}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Nonlinearities}
\label{sec:nonlinearities}

The design plant of \S\ref{sec:linearisation} drops every term that vanishes
at the equilibrium. The real machine retains them, and each imposes a
constraint on the recoverable envelope or introduces a signal the control law
must handle without amplifying.

\subsubsection{Torque saturation and the recoverable envelope}
\label{sec:saturation}

Two independent bounds limit the tilt from which the cube can recover, and
which one binds depends on a single hardware parameter.

\paragraph{Momentum bound.} Recovery from a tilt $\theta$ at rest requires
angular momentum
%
\begin{equation}
  h = \sqrt{2\,\bar\Theta\,S_g\,(1-\cos\theta)}\,,
\end{equation}
%
which must not exceed the stored capacity
$h_{\mathrm{cap}} = I_s\,\omega_{\mathrm{cap}} = 0.019475$\,N\,m\,s.
Inverting,
%
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{\max}^{\mathrm{mom}} =
  \arccos\!\left(1 - \frac{h_{\mathrm{cap}}^2}{2\,\bar\Theta\,S_g}\right)
  = 4.88^\circ .
  \label{eq:mom-bound}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Torque bound.} The controller can hold a static tilt only while
the gravitational moment does not exceed the available torque. At a corner
the worst-case actuator projection is $\sqrt{2}\,\tau_{\mathrm{cont}}$,
giving
%
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{\max}^{\mathrm{torq}} =
  \arcsin\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\tau_{\mathrm{cont}}}{S_g}\right)
  = 5.16^\circ \quad \text{at } \tau_{\mathrm{cont}} = 0.12\ \mathrm{N\,m}.
  \label{eq:torq-bound}
\end{equation}

The recoverable envelope is the smaller of the two. At the current hardware
point this is the momentum bound, but the margin is narrow.

$\pm\tau_{\mathrm{peak}} = \pm 0.40$\,N\,m. This figure derives from the

the ohmic dissipation is $1.5 I^2 R_\phi = 74.5$\,W per motor with
$R_\phi = 194$\,m$\Omega$, or $223$\,W across three motors, into a closed
volume.

\subsubsection{Wheel-speed cap}
\label{sec:wheel-cap}

Each wheel is capped at $\omega_{\mathrm{cap}} = 40$\,rad\,s$^{-1}$, which is
$4.5\,\%$ of the motor no-load speed of $883$\,rad\,s$^{-1}$. The cap is a
firmware policy rather than a hardware limit. Its purpose is to make the
momentum-limited failure mode reachable and testable during bring-up, rather
than a rare event occurring only at the envelope edge under a particular
disturbance sequence.

Implementation is a one-sided clamp:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{if } (\rho_k \ge +\omega_{\mathrm{cap}} \text{ and } u_k > 0)
  \text{ or }
  (\rho_k \le -\omega_{\mathrm{cap}} \text{ and } u_k < 0):
  \quad u_k = 0.
  \label{eq:wheel-cap}
\end{equation}
%
Torque that would accelerate the wheel further is zeroed; torque decelerating
it back toward the cap is permitted. A symmetric clamp would hold the wheel
at the cap and remove the actuator from the system.

\paragraph{Budget.} A full-envelope recovery consumes
$0.0155$\,N\,m\,s, equivalent to $31.8$\,rad\,s$^{-1}$ of wheel speed, or
$80\,\%$ of the cap. The remaining $20\,\%$ covers standing wheel speed from
estimator bias (\S\ref{sec:bias-sensitivity}) and from friction feedforward
error (\S\ref{sec:friction}) combined. Exceeding it at rest means the machine
saturates before reaching the envelope edge.

\subsubsection{Friction feedforward}
\label{sec:friction}

Coulomb friction at the wheel bearings applies a constant retarding torque
whose sign depends on the direction of rotation. Uncompensated, it enters the
plant as a constant disturbance and the regulator responds by holding a
standing wheel speed, consuming momentum budget continuously.

\paragraph{Structure.} The compensation is a smooth approximation of the sign
function applied per wheel:
%
\begin{equation}
  u_k = u_{\mathrm{LQR},k}
        + \tau_{cw}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\rho_k}{\varepsilon}\right)
        + b_w\,\rho_k ,
  \label{eq:feedforward}
\end{equation}
%
with $\varepsilon = 0.05$\,rad\,s$^{-1}$ setting the transition width. The
$\tanh$ reaches $0.9987$ of its asymptote at $3\varepsilon =
0.15$\,rad\,s$^{-1}$.

\paragraph{$\tanh$ rather than $\mathrm{sign}$.} All three wheels sit near
zero at equilibrium, so a $\mathrm{sign}$ function chatters between
$\pm\tau_{cw}$ at every crossing, injecting a square wave into the torque
command at the loop rate. The smooth form removes the discontinuity while
recovering $\mathrm{sign}$ behaviour within three transition widths.

\paragraph{Sensitivity.} Without feedforward the standing wheel speed is
$\tau_{cw}/|K_3|$:

placeholders pending a spin-down test; the pivot Coulomb torque
$\tau_{cp} = 0$ is a placeholder pending a breakaway test. No friction
measurement has been made on either stage. The sensitivity study establishing
the thresholds above was conducted against these placeholders, so it bounds
the tolerance rather than predicting the behaviour. The measurements are
listed in \S\ref{sec:open}.

\subsubsection{Cogging torque}
\label{sec:cogging}

The MN4006 motors have 12 pole pairs, producing torque ripple at $12\times$
the mechanical frequency. At the wheel cap this is $76$\,Hz, below the
Nyquist frequency of the $400$\,Hz loop and therefore representable in the
control signal.

No explicit cogging compensation is applied. The moteus field-oriented
control loop runs well above any cogging harmonic and absorbs most of the
ripple internally; the residual is a periodic disturbance to the body,
attenuated by the body inertia. The cogging frequency reaches
$f/2 = 200$\,Hz at a wheel speed of $105$\,rad\,s$^{-1}$, which is the
threshold above which raising $\omega_{\mathrm{cap}}$ would require the
residual amplitude to be characterised first. At the present cap of
$40$\,rad\,s$^{-1}$ there is a factor of $2.6$ in hand.

\subsubsection{Vibration and anti-aliasing}
\label{sec:vibration}

Three sources of high-frequency content reach the accelerometer.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Structural resonance} of the printed frame. Frequency
        unmeasured; expected above $200$\,Hz given the stiffness of PETG-CF
        at the $150$\,mm scale, but this has not been confirmed and is the
        assumption most likely to be challenged if the frame is lightened.
  \item \textbf{Cogging torque} at $12\times$ the wheel frequency
        (\S\ref{sec:cogging}).
  \item \textbf{Wheel unbalance}, producing a once-per-revolution forced
        vibration at $6.4$\,Hz at the cap. CAD places each wheel centre of
        mass on its spin axis to within $10^{-8}$\,mm, so the wheels are
        balanced by design; the as-built residual has not been measured. The
        sensitivity is $0.035$\,N of radial force per $0.1$\,mm of
        eccentricity at $40$\,rad\,s$^{-1}$, scaling as $\omega^2$.
\end{enumerate}

Content above $f/2 = 200$\,Hz folds into the estimator passband and is
indistinguishable from tilt. The complementary filter provides some
attenuation but is not designed as an anti-aliasing filter, and its rejection
above $200$\,Hz is finite.

This mechanism, not discrete stability, sets the binding lower bound on loop
rate: the anti-aliasing floor is $281$\,Hz against a stability floor of
$38.3$\,Hz (\S\ref{sec:loop-rate}).

\subsubsection{Per-cycle torque computation}
\label{sec:torque-computation}

Collecting \S\ref{sec:lqr} and the preceding subsections, the torque applied
to wheel $k$ on each cycle is
%
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  u_k = \mathrm{clamp}\!\Big(
    \underbrace{-\sum_{j=1}^{9} K_{p,kj}\,x_j}_{\text{LQR}}
    + \underbrace{\tau_{cw}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\rho_k}{\varepsilon}\right)
                  + b_w\rho_k}_{\text{friction feedforward}}\,,
    \;\pm\tau_{\mathrm{peak}}\Big)
  \;}
  \label{eq:torque-full}
\end{equation}
%
followed by the one-sided wheel cap of \eqref{eq:wheel-cap}. The ordering is
fixed: feedforward is added before the clamp, and the wheel cap is applied
after it. Reversing either produces a different controller.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C,caption={Per-cycle actuation, applied after the
estimator of \S\ref{sec:estimation} and the control law of
\S\ref{sec:lqr}.}]
for (int k = 0; k < 3; k++) {
    u[k] = 0.0f;
    for (int j = 0; j < 9; j++)
        u[k] -= Kp[k][j] * x[j];                // LQR

    u[k] += tau_cw * tanhf(rho[k] / eps_ff);     // Coulomb
    u[k] += b_w * rho[k];                        // viscous

    u[k] = clampf(u[k], -tau_peak, tau_peak);    // torque clamp

    if ((rho[k] >= +omega_cap && u[k] > 0.0f)    // wheel cap,
     || (rho[k] <= -omega_cap && u[k] < 0.0f))   // one-sided
        u[k] = 0.0f;
}
\end{lstlisting}

The error scales as $\theta_0^2$ to leading order, so period errors roughly
double the inertia error. On the panel this was caught because the
small-amplitude formula was validated against a finite-amplitude simulation
and the two disagreed by more than Gate~2's tolerance until the correction
was applied. The remedy is either to keep swings below $10^\circ$ (where the
error is under $0.4\,\%$) or to apply \eqref{eq:elliptic} and extract
$\bar\Theta$ from the corrected period.

\subsubsection{The return-difference identity}
\label{sec:return-difference}

For full-state linear quadratic regulation with the cost
$J = \int(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{x} + \bm{u}^{\top}\bm{R}\bm{u})\,
\mathrm{d}t$, the return-difference matrix satisfies
%
\begin{equation}
  M_s = \bar\sigma\!\left[(\bm{I} + \bm{L})^{-1}\right] = 1.000000000\,,
  \label{eq:Ms}
\end{equation}
%
where $\bm{L} = \bm{K}_r(s\bm{I} - \bm{A}_r)^{-1}\bm{B}_r$ is the loop
transfer of the reduced system. This is not a computed margin; it is the
identity of Kalman \cite{kalman1964optimal}, forced to hold exactly by the
algebraic structure of the Riccati equation. Any deviation from $1$ is an
implementation fault --- a wrong matrix, a non-positive-semidefinite $\bm{Q}$,
or a Riccati solution that did not converge --- rather than a property of the
design.

The identity serves as a single-number acceptance test on the gain
computation, catching classes of error that would otherwise require
inspecting a $3\times 9$ matrix entry by entry. It is evaluated on the
reduced system of \S\ref{sec:reduction}, where the Riccati problem is
well-posed, and not on the full nine-state system, where it is not
(\cref{tab:riccati}).

\paragraph{Scope.} Three things the identity does not cover.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Discretisation.} Discretising the reduced-order LQR at
        $400$\,Hz gives $M_s = 1.039$, a $3.9\,\%$ deviation. The guarantee
        does not survive the zero-order hold \cite{doyle1978guaranteed}, and
        discrete margins must be verified directly against the $z$-plane
        eigenvalues (\S\ref{sec:loop-rate}).
  \item \textbf{An estimator in the loop.} Replacing the full state with the
        output of \S\ref{sec:estimation} breaks the loop transfer on which
        the identity is defined, and Doyle's counter-example
        \cite{doyle1978guaranteed} shows that LQG can have arbitrarily small
        margins. The return-difference identity is a statement about the
        \emph{controller}, not about the closed loop with the estimator
        included.
  \item \textbf{Gain structure.} $M_s = 1$ confirms that the gain is optimal
        for the stated cost, but the cost may be wrong. The sign-pattern and
        uniformity checks of \S\ref{sec:gains} test the gain's physical
        plausibility independently of the cost function.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Tolerance in rank computations}
\label{sec:rank-trap}

The controllability argument of \S\ref{sec:structural} rests on a rank count.
An incorrect tolerance can invert the conclusion silently.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Call} & \textbf{Result}\\
\midrule
\texttt{rank(Co)} (default, relative tolerance) & $8$ --- correct\\
\texttt{rank(Co, 1e-9)} (absolute tolerance) & $9$ --- incorrect\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
$\lVert\mathcal{C}\rVert = 1.105\times10^{9}$, so an absolute tolerance of
$10^{-9}$ is $9\times10^{-19}$ of the matrix scale --- below the
double-precision floor. Every singular value, including the one at
$9.19\times10^{-17}$ that is numerically zero, exceeds that threshold.

The failure reports the system as fully controllable, which requires no
further action. Had the result been accepted, the eight-state reduction
(\S\ref{sec:reduction}) would not have been written, \texttt{lqr} would have
returned a gain without complaint (\cref{tab:riccati}), and the first
indication of a problem would have arrived on hardware as unexplained
authority loss in a direction that cannot be identified from the symptoms.

A tolerance must be chosen relative to the scale of the matrix it is applied
to. MATLAB's default does this; a hand-supplied absolute figure carried from
another problem does not. The general form: tolerances that are safe for one
matrix may be orders of magnitude too loose or too tight for the next one,
and the failure mode in both cases is silence.

$15$\,s or check the eigenvalues directly.
